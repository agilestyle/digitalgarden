Nushin Yazdani's work through Superrr Lab reframed something for me that I'd been circling without finding the right language for.

The dominant frame in AI governance is risk mitigation: identify the ways a system can cause harm, build safeguards, document them, comply with the regulation. It's a defensive posture. And it tends to be designed around the concerns of institutions — what liabilities can be managed, what scrutiny can be survived.

A feminist approach to AI governance starts somewhere different: from the lived experience of communities that have historically been harmed by both technology and by institutions designed to "protect" them. It asks not "how do we reduce risk?" but "what would this look like if it was designed for people who have least reason to trust it?"

That inversion changes everything. Which risks get named. Who gets to define harm. What accountability actually looks like in practice.

This is in conversation with [[responsible-for-whom]] in my notes — the question of who "responsible AI" is being responsible toward. Superrr Lab makes that question undeniable.

I'm also struck by the attention to care in feminist tech frameworks. Care as a design principle — not aesthetics, not efficiency, but sustaining attention to the needs of others over time. That feels like something the broader field could use.
