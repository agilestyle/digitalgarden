---
title: Can consent survive scale?
date: 2026-03-03
tags: [consent, governance, surveillance, open-question, clearview]
---


Clearview AI collected 30 billion images. No consent was sought. No consent could have been sought — the mechanics of scraping the open web at that scale make individual notice and agreement structurally impossible.

The standard data protection response is: then you can't do it. GDPR, CCPA, various court rulings in different countries have all said some version of this. But Clearview did do it, and continues to operate in most of the world, and the faces remain indexed.

This is an open question for me: consent frameworks were designed for contexts where the number of parties is bounded and relationships are traceable. They assume a data subject who can be identified, notified, and who retains some meaningful choice. At the scale of facial recognition trained on public data, none of those conditions hold.

So what replaces consent when consent doesn't scale?

Some possibilities:
- **Categorical prohibition**: certain data types (biometrics, faces) are treated as too sensitive to collect without explicit opt-in, regardless of scale — the data simply can't exist
- **Collective governance**: communities, not individuals, hold rights over images made in their spaces — a neighbourhood, a city, a platform
- **Mandatory public benefit**: systems like this are only permitted where the entity accountable for them accepts liability for harms and demonstrates clear public benefit — not commercial benefit

I don't know which of these is right, or whether any of them is enforceable at the speed technology moves. But I notice that the current consent framework mostly transfers the burden to the individual — opt out if you can, complain if you're harmed — which at scale means no burden at all.
