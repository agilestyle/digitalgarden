Term II had a module called "From Principles to Implementation" and I underestimated how hard that gap would be to close.

It's easy to agree on principles. Transparency, fairness, accountability, privacy — read any AI ethics framework from any government or company and they're all in there. The principles aren't the problem. The problem is that they're designed to be agreeable and therefore vague enough to absorb almost any practice.

What does fairness mean when it's operationalised? Which metric do you choose, and why? Whose definition of harm are you using? Who's in the room when these decisions get made?

Module 2.2 pushed us to actually try to implement responsible AI guidelines in specific contexts. That exercise exposed the friction immediately. The principles float above the context; implementation is always context-specific. There's no universal playbook.

I keep coming back to the word "explainability." It's technically precise — can you trace why this system made this decision? — but it's being deployed in a political context where explanation is often confused with justification. Explaining how a discriminatory system works doesn't make it less discriminatory.

This is the gap I'm most interested in: between what the field says it values and what it actually builds.
