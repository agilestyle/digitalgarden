---
title: A face isn't just data
tags: [surveillance, identity, futures, pluriversal, term-3]
---


Working through the Clearview assignment pushed me toward a question I hadn't thought to ask: what does it mean that a face is *permanent*?

Other kinds of personal data can be revoked, updated, or deleted — a password, an address, a preference. A face can't. The moment your image was scraped and embedded, a version of you entered a permanent index. You didn't consent to it. You can't remove it. You can age, change, move countries — the embedding persists.

There's an intergenerational problem here that I find particularly uncomfortable. Children photographed and posted online in the last decade didn't consent to anything — they couldn't. They're adults now, and their faces have been searchable for years without their knowledge. The parents who posted those photos made a decision whose consequences extend far beyond their own lives.

I kept coming back to a frame from the futures work we did in Term II: what if we thought about identity as something *custodial* rather than individual? Not "my face, my data, my choice" — but "this image was made in a community context and carries obligations to that community." Some cultures already operate this way. Many Indigenous communities have practices around portraiture, naming, and image that recognise the communal and temporal dimensions of identity representation.

That reframe doesn't solve the Clearview problem. But it does reveal something about the assumptions baked into our dominant data frameworks — that identity is individual property, transferable and sellable by whoever currently holds the bits.

The surveillance imagination that produced Clearview AI assumes ambient, persistent, universal legibility. The alternative imagination I find more interesting is *episodic* surveillance: visibility in specific contexts, for specific purposes, governed by the community being observed. That's not a technical architecture. It's a political and cultural one.
